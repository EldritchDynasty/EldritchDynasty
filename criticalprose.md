# Critical Prose

> Internal narrative reference. This document contains campaign, ending, and identity spoilers.
>
> Editorial guidance and illustrative beats, reviewed against `main` at
> `098bfe02` on 22 October 2026. Examples and desired emotional effects are not
> guarantees of event reach or evidence from players. The live YAML and
> [fullstory.md](fullstory.md) describe the shipped story; #1867 tracks the
> further founder identity and attachment work.

## Purpose

This document records the story elements and prose beats that most directly make Eldritch Dynasty hard to put down.

It is not another rules specification and it should not duplicate the schema or the implementation. Its job is narrower:

- identify the story promises the game makes to the player;
- identify the questions that pull the player from one scene, generation, Age, and campaign beat into the next;
- preserve the prose moments that make those questions emotionally expensive;
- show how sub-stories reconnect to the bloodline, Ledger, and chronicle instead of becoming decorative side content;
- give authors a practical test for the final line of a scene: what does the player now want to know or do?

The central principle is simple:

> The player should nearly always finish one answer while acquiring a better question.

The game is generational, so the most powerful hook is not “what happens in the next room?” It is:

> What will this choice look like when somebody who was not born yet has to live with it?

---

## 1. The narrative north star

The structural thesis is:

> This is the true story of how the house became the thing people sing about — told by the last of the line, on the last night, to the creditor sitting across the table.

The emotional thesis is:

> Everything after this was done by people who did not sign it.

Those two lines contain the whole game.

The first creates the forward question: how does an ordinary, compromised house become a legend?

The second creates the moral pressure: every generation inherits an agreement it did not make, then makes new agreements for descendants who did not consent either.

The player is therefore never merely building a dynasty. The player is producing three things at once:

1. a bloodline;
2. a debt history;
3. a version of events.

The interesting story is the disagreement between them.

If a major scene, Age, arc, or system does not touch blood, the Ledger, or the record, it needs a strong reason to exist.

## 2. The story in one page

### 1042: the signing

On the last of the Hollow Days, the founder sits in a hall too large for the family that owns it.

Something comes in without being seen.

The room reacts to it: a dog leaves and will not return, iron dulls, wet ash is smelled where it should not be, and the witnesses cannot agree how long the encounter lasted.

Three things are given:

- the roof — the house's claim and continued holding;
- the thing in the blood — the family-exclusive Eldritch inheritance;
- the keeping — three founding objects that must remain in a hand that can be shown.

Three things are owed:

- a page a year;
- the sons;
- the term.

The founder treats centuries as a number because he will not personally have to endure them.

That is the first sin of the dynasty and the template for almost everything the player will later do.

### The player

The player is not one mortal character. The player is the will of the line.

Internally, the implementation makes this literal: the player names the first character and first Head (Daveed Gearithy by default). When death reaches him it is redirected; he becomes the guardian spirit of the house and remains castable forever. The player has been him all along. The authored founding body remains male; #1867's identity/configuration extension is not shipped.

That fact should remain a story advantage rather than casual exposition. It gives the entire campaign a second reading: the founder who signed the debt never truly leaves the family alone with it.

### The campaign

A Short Line runs from 1042 to 1342. A Long Line runs from 1042 to 1542.

Across generations the player:

- chooses marriages;
- concentrates or dilutes the blood;
- raises children who may or may not awaken;
- spends family members on careers, politics, rites, and survival;
- manages respectability while power makes respectability harder;
- chooses what the chronicle records, omits, or embellishes;
- recovers pieces of the agreement;
- climbs the Ascension Ladder;
- leaves problems for people not yet born.

The player is always working toward a more capable descendant, but the game must never let “more capable” collapse into “better.”

Power creates exposure.
Truth can destroy Respect.
Lies preserve status but poison the record.
Knowledge can solve an old problem and make the contract worse.

### The last night

At the term, the chronicle is brought to the table.

The creditor reads the book the player spent the campaign making.

Not the hidden world state.
Not the player's intentions.
The book.

The ending is therefore not simply “did the family become strong enough?” It is the collision of:

- what happened;
- what survived;
- what was written;
- what can still be proven;
- who is left to sit at the table.

## 3. The five campaign questions

These are the questions the game should keep alive for most of a run.

### 3.1 What did the founder actually agree to?

The prologue gives enough to act and not enough to understand.

Each recovered clause should narrow the answer while making the shape of the agreement less comfortable.

The Ledger is strongest when knowledge is not a reward screen. It is a liability arriving in legible form.

### 3.2 What is the other party?

This is a cosmic question and must not be closed in the game's own voice.

The player may learn what it is not.
The player may learn that an old interpretation was wrong.
The player may learn that the contract language itself is inadequate.

The player should never receive a villain monologue that turns the other party into a comprehensible person with a plan.

### 3.3 What is the blood turning the family into?

The Ascension Ladder makes this mechanical, but the story question is larger.

Each rung should make the player ask whether the family is:

- improving;
- deforming;
- fulfilling the contract;
- becoming the payment;
- or becoming something able to stand on the other side of it.

### 3.4 Which version of the family will survive?

The chronicle turns every important event into a second decision.

The player does not only ask, “What should we do?”

The player also asks, “What should our descendants believe we did?”

The contradiction between those two answers is the game's most reusable narrative engine.

### 3.5 Who will be sitting at the table?

Every birth, death, marriage, Regency, sacrifice, branch split, and succession quietly edits the cast of the final night.

The frame makes the last descendant present from the beginning even when the player does not know their name.

That is why a death in 1120 can matter to a scene in 1542.

## 4. The three story layers

The register split is not cosmetic. It tells the player what kind of truth they are currently hearing.

| Layer | Function | Voice | Hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Frame | The bill has arrived | elevated, quiet, mythic | How does the present become this room? |
| The Tale | The lived campaign | warm, concrete, particular | What do I do with this family now? |
| Nested Tales | What the world says happened | biased, mythic, contradictory | Which version is useful, dangerous, or true enough? |

### The Frame

The frame is the promise.

Two figures.
A long table.
A cold house.
The chronicle between them.

It should be quieter than the campaign and should react to the record rather than explain systems.

Every interlude should worsen or complicate one visible fact in the room:

- fewer fires;
- a missing chair;
- a page marked;
- an heirloom absent;
- a clause already selected;
- a name the descendant refuses to read aloud;
- evidence that the creditor noticed something the family thought it buried.

The frame's job is to convert the player's recent past into future dread.

### The Tale

The Tale is where warmth lives.

Rooms should be worth returning to.
Family members should be funny, petty, loyal, vain, frightened, useful, irritating, and missed.
A hall should sometimes be full.

If the player does not love anything temporary, the later losses are accounting.

The Tale also owns the ordinary decision rhythm: matches, births, feuds, careers, ceremonies, illnesses, scandals, and small acts of selfishness that become history because a descendant has to pay for them.

### Nested Tales

Nested tales are not lore dumps.

They are evidence with fingerprints on it.

A sermon, rival chronicle, children's rhyme, tavern song, court testimony, or provincial play must belong to someone with a stake.

Two accounts of the same event should disagree.

The game should not adjudicate the disagreement in its own voice.

A good nested tale creates at least one of these reactions:

- “That is not what happened.”
- “That is what I wrote, but not what happened.”
- “How do they know that?”
- “Why did this version survive?”
- “Which version will my descendants act on?”

## 5. The hook ladder

Eldritch Dynasty needs hooks at several timescales because one campaign spans centuries.

### Immediate hook — the next sentence

Use a concrete disturbance.

Something is wrong with the room, object, document, person, or custom before the text explains why.

Examples of the shape:

- a letter has been opened but its seal is intact;
- a dog refuses a room it has slept in for years;
- a family book contains a page in the wrong hand;
- a candidate knows a private family name;
- an heirloom is warm in winter;
- a priest quotes a sentence the family omitted.

The immediate hook should be legible before it is explainable.

### Scene hook — the next decision

A scene should quickly become somebody's problem.

The player should understand:

- what is happening;
- who will pay if nothing is done;
- what the available choices reveal about the family.

A choice is strongest when every option says something different about what the player values.

### Generation hook — the next heir

A generation should end with unfinished inheritance.

The player should leave thinking about at least one future consequence:

- the child who carries the better blood but the worse risk;
- the lie that will become family doctrine;
- the branch that was slighted;
- the heirloom that was loaned and not returned;
- the clause that changed what an earlier choice meant;
- the person who is too young to matter yet.

### Age hook — the next interpretation

An Age must pay its story debts:

- a Ledger fact;
- a standing change;
- a Rumour.

The Age ending should therefore change the player's model of the campaign.

Do not merely raise stakes. Change meaning.

### Campaign hook — the last night

The whole run is played against one deferred scene.

The player knows from the beginning that the book will be read back.

That turns every Record, Omit, and Embellish choice into an appointment.

## 6. The “what do I do next?” drive

The game should repeatedly convert story information into a decision or plan.

| Story beat | Question created | What the player does next | Future promise |
|---|---|---|---|
| The signing | What was really agreed? | Name the house, choose the founding legacy, begin the line | The term will arrive |
| A promising child | Is this the heir we have been breeding for? | educate, protect, marry, awaken, or risk them | their children reveal whether the bet worked |
| A bad Awakening | Can power be kept without destroying the bearer? | contain, conceal, force, abandon, or redirect | madness and Respect return later |
| A marriage offer | What are we importing into the bloodline? | pick among blood, politics, dowry, and hidden risk | secrets and inheritance surface later |
| A recovered clause | What did we misunderstand? | revise priorities, preserve records, pursue the next clue | another clause may invalidate this reading |
| A damaging truth | Can the house afford to remember this? | Record, Omit, or Embellish | descendants inherit truth, absence, or a lie |
| An embellishment | Who can prove us wrong? | protect status, destroy evidence, outlive witnesses, or accept exposure | Discrepancy and scandal |
| A Regency | Can the line survive without advancing? | preserve the house and engineer the next eligible heir | the Ledger keeps counting |
| A rival's accusation | Are they lying, or did our own book lie first? | investigate, answer, suppress, bargain | competing records survive |
| A sacrifice gate | Who is the family willing to spend? | choose a real relative | absence remains in tree and record |
| A missing heirloom | Who has it now? | negotiate, marry, steal back politically, trade, or wait | Demigod gate remains blocked |
| A frame interlude | What in our recent history caused this? | return to the campaign with a new suspicion | the last night grows more specific |
| A nested tale | Why does their version differ? | decide whether to believe, exploit, or refute it | the legend may outlive the truth |
| A branch grievance | Will this cousin become the rescue or the wound? | reconcile, reward, marry, ignore, or prepare | succession can make them central |
| A new rung | What will the next rung require us to become? | build the next generation around the cost | ascension intensifies the moral price |

The important transition is:

> Revelation -> interpretation -> decision -> deferred consequence.

If a revelation produces no altered decision, it is lore.
If a decision produces no deferred consequence, it is a menu.

## 7. The critical story elements that must survive cuts

If production scope forces narrative cuts, protect these before secondary lore.

### 7.1 The debt of three parts

The announced triad is the spine.

Three things given.
Three things owed.

Every ending rings it by changing exactly one element.

That structural echo is more important than adding another isolated mystery.

### 7.2 The chronicle as evidence

The chronicle must remain player-authored and consequential.

Record, Omit, and Embellish are not flavour variants.
They determine what descendants know and what the creditor later reads.

The chronicle is where the story's theme becomes an action.

### 7.3 The frame

Without the last night, the campaign loses its promise.

The frame does not need much volume. It needs function.

It must periodically remind the player that:

- the term is real;
- the book survives;
- somebody is reading toward them.

### 7.4 The blood law

The story depends on the inheritance rule creating social consequences.

Eldritch Power is X-linked and male-expressed.
Women carry it without expressing it.
Mystic magic follows a different rule.
Madness belongs only to those capable of expressing Eldritch Power.

This is not trivia. It drives marriage, succession, Regency, scandal, risk, and the contradiction between who carries the future and who is legally allowed to lead it.

### 7.5 The Ascension Ladder

The Ladder gives the centuries a visible direction.

Touched -> Adept -> Hierophant -> The Vessel -> Demigod -> God.

Each rung should feel less like “level up” and more like “the family has crossed a line it once would have recognised as terrible.”

### 7.6 The other party's incomprehensibility

The other party must remain larger than a villain.

No motive speech.
No grievance.
No explained plan.

The horror is strongest if the founder thought this was a bargain between persons and the campaign never proves that assumption.

### 7.7 The ending ring

The ending must make the prologue newly legible.

A good ending does not introduce the game's meaning.
It reveals what the opening line had been doing for centuries.

## 8. Sub-story engines

Sub-stories should not pause the main plot. They should put pressure on it from a human angle.

### 8.1 The five unnamed friends

The founder is asked to name five people outside his blood without whom his life would have been another life.

The names are then lost.

Versions of them return across generations, slightly better than they have any business being, until the pattern stops.

This is an unusually strong sub-story because it creates all three hooks at once:

- recognition: “is this one of them?”
- uncertainty: nobody in the current family knows the rule;
- absence: the returns stop, which becomes more disturbing than another return.

Protect the silence after the fifth.

### 8.2 Founding grudges

Marrow, Calder, and Ilm are good models for inherited conflict because each remembers differently.

- Marrow acquires and reads.
- Calder remembers a betrayal attached to the chair rather than the person.
- Ilm writes patiently and shares the writing.

A grudge is strongest when it has a behaviour, not merely a hostility score.

The player should learn to fear what a rival does with memory.

### 8.3 Marriage secrets

The Match is a generational cliffhanger machine.

A candidate should never be only a bundle of stats.

Each match can open:

- a blood question;
- a political question;
- a family question;
- a secret that becomes visible only after the marriage mattered.

The ideal feeling is: “I know why I chose them, and I am no longer sure that was the important thing about them.”

### 8.4 Cadet branches

Cadet branches turn discarded heirs into future plot.

A cousin passed over now can become:

- a loyal auxiliary;
- a grievance carrier;
- a succession rescue;
- a rival centre of legitimacy;
- the only surviving branch when the main line fails.

The branch system makes “not chosen” different from “irrelevant.”

Use it.

### 8.5 The recurring cast slots

Write multi-generation arcs around roles that refill:

- the Mentor Who Is Taken Away;
- the Institutional Rival;
- the Fragile One;
- the Unattainable;
- the Found Family;
- the Authority Board.

The hook comes from rhyme.

The player should feel that the family keeps meeting the same human problem in a new face and keeps discovering whether it learned anything.

### 8.6 Discrepancies

An embellishment is a delayed antagonist authored by the player.

The ideal Discrepancy arc has four beats:

1. the lie is useful;
2. the lie becomes conventional;
3. somebody acts on it as though it were true;
4. somebody with another record arrives.

The fourth beat should feel inevitable in hindsight and surprising in timing.

### 8.7 Lost knowledge and blank lines

Omission should create visible absence.

A dated blank line is stronger than a generic “knowledge lost” notification because it lets the player imagine what future readers see.

A blank is a promise that the missing thing can matter again.

### 8.8 The Ledger clauses

Every clause must be a fact, not a tease.

But the fact should destabilise the player's model.

Good clause progression:

- confirms there are terms;
- exposes a missing part of the family's account;
- shows a term behaving unlike a normal term;
- invalidates a comfortable interpretation;
- makes the agreement larger than the family.

Each new clause should create a strategy question.

### 8.9 Regency

A Regency is not downtime.

It is a clock problem.

The house can defend, enrich, arrange, and survive while the ladder does not advance and the Ledger does.

That creates a different kind of urgency: a generation can be successful and still lose time.

### 8.10 The sacrifice

The Vessel rite should remain personal.

Do not let it become a resource icon.

The player chooses a person from the tree.

The story value is not the power gained. It is that the chosen person remains absent in every scene and branch that follows.

### 8.11 The heirlooms

The three founding objects are campaign memory made physical.

They should repeatedly answer two questions:

- where is it?
- whose hand can be shown holding it?

An heirloom story is useful when possession reveals a relationship: marriage, inheritance, betrayal, debt, branch legitimacy, or a promise that survived its maker.

### 8.12 The frame interludes

An interlude should not say, “meanwhile, in 1542.”

It should answer a recent event with a changed detail at the table.

The best interlude hook is recognition without explanation.

The player sees the scar before learning which choice made it.

## 9. Prose responsibilities by surface

Different surfaces have different jobs. Do not make every screen sound equally important.

### Start screen / pitch

Job: create the impossible time-scale and the debt in as few lines as possible.

The existing pitch is already the correct model:

> Five hundred years is just enough time to ruin a family properly.

Then:

> In year 1042 your ancestor signed something. In 1542 the other party comes to collect.

The hook is not lore. It is a deadline.

### Prologue

Job: bind beauty, wrongness, structure, and debt before the player knows the systems.

The prologue should:

- establish the house as too large and too empty;
- describe the visitor by effects rather than anatomy;
- announce the triad;
- make each gift more consequential;
- make each price worse on reflection;
- end on the plain thesis.

The prologue is allowed to be mythic because it is the source of the myth.

### Ordinary event opening

Job: put the player in a room and disturb one concrete thing.

Prefer:

- sound;
- temperature;
- absence;
- an object behaving wrongly;
- a social custom breaking.

Do not open by explaining the theme of the event.

### Choice text

Job: make values legible.

Choices should differ in what they protect:

- blood;
- person;
- money;
- truth;
- Respect;
- time;
- branch loyalty;
- future optionality.

If two choices protect the same thing at different prices, the scene may be an optimisation problem rather than a story decision.

### Outcome text

Job: pay the immediate result and leave the delayed cost alive.

The player should know what just happened.

The player should not always know what it will mean.

### Chronicle prompt

Job: make the player reinterpret the event they just survived.

Record, Omit, Embellish works because it asks a second moral question after the tactical one.

The prose should make the distinction concrete:

- what descendants will know;
- what the public will believe;
- what evidence now exists.

### Ledger clause

Job: provide one hard fact and damage one interpretation.

A clause should not end “mysteriously.”

It should end precisely enough that the player changes a plan.

### Nested tale

Job: create useful contradiction.

The teller and bias matter as much as the content.

A nested tale should make the player compare sources, not consume lore passively.

### Age transition

Job: announce that the world now behaves differently.

Name Ages late.
Let the player live the change before the chronicle labels it.

The title should feel like a verdict on years already experienced.

### Frame interlude

Job: make the future room worse.

Slow down.
Reduce explanation.
Let the book, house, descendant, and creditor carry the dread.

### Ending

Job: ring the opening.

Do not invent a new metaphorical system in the last page.

Return to:

- the house;
- the book;
- the blood;
- the three-part debt;
- the plain closing line.

## 10. The two prose registers

### 10.1 The Tale: concrete, warm, sharp

Ordinary events should be plain and particular.

Core techniques:

- sound and temperature before sight;
- describe by absence;
- strong verbs;
- hard variation in sentence length;
- finish paragraphs on a short concrete line;
- use threes only for structural emphasis;
- avoid explaining the line that already landed.

The Tale should feel close enough to care about a kitchen, a wet cloak, a bad tooth, a child's handwriting, a cousin arriving too early, or a chair left empty.

The cosmic story becomes powerful only because these things matter first.

### 10.2 The myth layer: beautiful, elevated, withholding

The frame, prologue, Ledger, nested tales, Age blurbs, and endings can carry the elevated register.

Core techniques:

- longer accumulating sentences;
- mythic distance;
- beauty that is temporary;
- the incomprehensible described by effects;
- contradiction left unresolved;
- no stated cosmic motive;
- knowledge that makes the reader less comfortable rather than more secure.

The tonal progression should move broadly through:

1. what a strange and lovely thing this is;
2. what is this, actually;
3. was any of it ever meant for people?

Myth first.
Horror later.

## 11. High-value hook patterns

These are reusable structures, not mandatory wording.

### 11.1 The object that knows too much

A familiar object contains information it should not have.

Use for:

- heirlooms;
- letters;
- chronicles;
- seals;
- portraits;
- account books.

Pattern:

1. establish the object as ordinary;
2. reveal one impossible or private detail;
3. force a decision before fully explaining it.

### 11.2 The social rule that suddenly has a cost

A custom the family has relied on becomes the trap.

Use for:

- succession;
- marriage;
- Regency;
- Church doctrine;
- hospitality;
- inheritance.

Pattern:

1. remind the player of the rule through action;
2. present the exceptional person the rule harms;
3. make the player either enforce the rule or pay to break it.

### 11.3 The old choice returning in a new generation

This is the signature Eldritch Dynasty hook.

Pattern:

1. show the consequence before naming the cause;
2. let the player recognise the old decision;
3. reveal that descendants have a different version of why it happened;
4. require a fresh choice.

### 11.4 The useful lie

Pattern:

1. truth is expensive now;
2. embellishment solves the immediate problem;
3. the lie is repeated by someone who was not there;
4. the player realises the lie has become infrastructure.

### 11.5 The wrong answer that worked

A family explanation can be false and still useful for generations.

That makes later revelation painful instead of merely educational.

Pattern:

1. give the player a model that predicts events well enough to trust;
2. let them build policy around it;
3. introduce one fact that the model cannot survive;
4. do not replace it immediately with a complete new model.

### 11.6 The missing thing

Absence is one of the cheapest and strongest hooks in the project.

Use:

- no fire in six grates;
- a blank chronicle line;
- a branch no longer represented at table;
- an heirloom missing from the display;
- a name removed from a genealogy;
- the sixth expected return that never comes.

The player supplies the dread.

## 12. Suggested micro-prose for authoring direction

These are examples of hook shape, not new canon.

### Ordinary event opening

> The messenger had mud to his knees and no horse. He asked for the Head by name, then looked at the family tree on the wall and asked why the dead man was still listed first.

Why it hooks:

- concrete arrival;
- immediate social wrongness;
- a question tied to the family's hidden identity.

### Chronicle consequence

> The entry was copied cleanly. Forty-three years later, the copy was the only version anybody could find.

Why it hooks:

- immediate act;
- time jump;
- future authority given to the player's wording.

### Marriage secret

> Her dowry arrived before she did. One chest held silver, one held books, and the third was locked from the inside.

Why it hooks:

- conventional expectation;
- escalating triad;
- concrete impossible detail;
- obvious desire to continue.

### Cadet-branch return

> They had sent the cousin away with three horses and a grievance. He came back with twenty men and the only male child in the family who could wake.

Why it hooks:

- old decision;
- changed power relationship;
- immediate succession problem.

### Ledger recovery

> The clause did not say the family would pay at the end of the term. It said the account would be read then. Nobody in the archive could find a sentence that said those were the same event.

Why it hooks:

- hard textual fact;
- invalidates a comfortable assumption;
- creates strategic uncertainty.

### Frame interlude

> The book has acquired three ribbons since last time. The descendant recognises two of them. The third marks a page the family left blank.

Why it hooks:

- quiet change;
- link to player-authored omission;
- evidence the creditor can read absence.

## 13. The final-line test

Before approving a scene, identify the question its final line creates.

Good final-line questions include:

- Who knows?
- Who lied?
- Who inherits this?
- What did we miss?
- Which child can carry it?
- What will the rival do with this?
- Why is the record different?
- Where did the heirloom go?
- What does the clause rule out?
- What will this cost when the term arrives?
- What happens if this person becomes Head?
- What will our descendants think happened?

Weak final-line questions include:

- Will the number go up?
- Will another random event happen?
- What does this lore entry mean in general?
- Was that spooky?

If the scene ends with no active question, the prose may be finished while the story is not.

---

## 14. The scene-to-sub-story bridge

Every substantial event should leave at least one reusable thread.

Possible thread outputs:

- a named grievance;
- a person promoted into a recurring slot;
- a secret known by a rival;
- a book created, lost, or altered;
- an heirloom moved;
- a branch relationship changed;
- a Discrepancy created;
- a Rumour launched;
- a clause interpretation changed;
- a new absence in the family;
- a promise to a child;
- a fact the chronicle gets wrong.

The next scene does not need to continue the thread immediately.

In fact, a gap of years is often better.

The trick is to make the later return specific enough that the player remembers making the earlier choice.

---

## 15. The generation-ending test

A generation should rarely close cleanly.

Before succession, ask whether at least three of these remain alive:

- one person the player cares about;
- one unresolved family tension;
- one future genetic bet;
- one record choice with delayed consequences;
- one external relationship that changed;
- one Ledger question;
- one thing missing;
- one reason to fear or desire the next heir.

The player should click into the next generation because the previous one made the future personal.

---

## 16. The Age-ending test

Each Age should leave the player with:

1. one thing they now know;
2. one thing they can no longer believe;
3. one thing the house materially gained or lost;
4. one story the outside world now tells about them;
5. one practical priority for the next Age.

That fifth item is essential.

Mystery without changed behaviour becomes a wiki.

## 17. The ending afterimages

The current endings work because they do not merely report success or failure. They reinterpret the contract.

### Apotheosis

The family produces something that belongs on the other side of the agreement.

The question becomes whether ascent fulfilled the bargain or replaced one party to it.

### The Unmade

The blood's impossible thing is removed.

The house survives in a quieter, ordinary form.

The loss is also release.

### The Broken Line

The debt outlives the debtors.

The empty-house reading makes the contract feel procedural rather than vengeful.

### The Settled Account

The most literal victory is not domination but completion.

The debt is paid and the family is left with every ordinary problem that was never part of the contract.

### The Forgotten

The family kept the book and failed to become cosmically important.

This is essential because it prevents the setting from flattering the dynasty merely for being the player's dynasty.

### The Devoured

Formidability attracts rather than protects.

The family's proudest achievement becomes the reason the final encounter is interested in it.

Across all endings, preserve the plain closing-line drop. Elevated prose makes the room mythic; the simple sentence makes the consequence human.

---

## 18. Pride and the two-generation bill

Pride is strongest when it is not named as a meter in prose.

The player's bearing is visible through acts:

- whom they spend;
- what they conceal;
- who they think is beneath the line;
- what risks they accept because a descendant is exceptional;
- whether they treat the record as evidence or advertising.

The crucial story technique is delayed billing.

A proud act should often feel effective in the generation where it is made.

The bill should arrive later, ideally through somebody who did not make the choice.

That is how theme becomes inheritance rather than authorial commentary.

## 19. What not to do

### Do not explain the cosmic tier

The other party stops being frightening when it becomes a quest NPC with motives.

### Do not make every event mythic

The campaign needs kitchens, marriages, debts, weather, petty insults, sick children, career opportunities, and full halls.

The myth layer works because it interrupts ordinary life.

### Do not make every consequence immediate

A generational game should exploit time.

Some of the best payoffs belong fifty or one hundred years after the cause.

### Do not make every old choice return

Recurrence needs scarcity.

If every minor decision comes back, return itself stops feeling meaningful.

### Do not confuse obscurity with mystery

The player should understand the current problem.

Mystery belongs to meaning, motive, history, and future cost — not to basic scene comprehension.

### Do not let lore answer a question the player never asked

Open the question first.
Then pay it.

### Do not let the chronicle become a cosmetic recap

If the book cannot hurt, help, deceive, preserve, expose, or alter the ending, the central thesis collapses.

### Do not let high power erase human stakes

No cosmic revelation should make an earlier death, marriage, betrayal, sacrifice, or kindness trivial in the game's own voice.

The family can become enormous in consequence without the people becoming small in meaning.

## 20. Critical prose checklist

Before shipping a major narrative beat:

### Story

- Does it touch blood, Ledger, or record?
- What active question does it open?
- What existing question does it narrow or close?
- What does the player do differently because of it?
- What can return in a later generation?
- Does a human relationship carry part of the cost?

### Ordinary event prose

- Is the opening concrete?
- Is sound, temperature, or absence doing useful work?
- Is the rhythm varied?
- Is the final sentence short enough to land?
- Did we explain the line after it already worked?
- Are the choices about values, not merely prices?

### Myth/frame prose

- Is there beauty before dread?
- Is the beautiful thing temporary?
- Is the incomprehensible described by effects rather than anatomy?
- Did we accidentally give the other party a motive?
- Did the passage narrow possibilities without over-explaining?
- Does the frame react to the player's record?

### Continuation

- What does the player want to know next?
- What do they want to do next?
- Are those two desires connected?

If the last two answers are both weak, the beat needs another pass.
