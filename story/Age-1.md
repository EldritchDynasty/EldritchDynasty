# AGE ONE — the first thing the reader reads

**Status:** authored narrative reference, not a literal runtime transcript.
Reviewed for provenance against `main` at `098bfe02` on 22 October 2026. The
signing now includes founder naming and the Examination, and its term is
campaign-aware. Use the live `packages/content/prologue.yaml`, Age/event YAML
and [fullstory.md](../fullstory.md) for current text and scheduling; the sequence
below preserves the earlier composition and editorial notes.

**Reading order.** Everything below is in the order the player meets it, from the moment the game
opens to the moment the first Age is named and closes. Prose is final-draft; the bracketed notes
are for the build and are not shown.

**Register.** Sections I–V, VII–XI are **Dunsanian** — the frame, the prologue, the clause, the
interlude, the rumour, the Age blurbs. Section VI is **Rothfuss**, and the change of voice is the
point: when the prose goes plain, the player has entered the ledger; when it goes ornate, they have
entered the myth, and the myth is the layer that lies.

**Canon this is written to.** Ardwen on the Wend, seven grates, eleven farms, Wick at 120 souls.
The founder is named nowhere — the chronicle does not use his name and neither does this. The
`{HOUSE}` token is what the player types in section III. The Clause of the Term is quoted verbatim
from `packages/content/clauses.yaml`. The guardian is `LISTENER_B` and takes *he*; the last of the
blood is `LISTENER_A` and takes no pronoun at all, per `events/frame.yaml`.

## I. THE OPENING — year 2042

*No music. Two colours. The text arrives slower than the player expects and they cannot speed it.*

> The house was quiet on the last night, and it was a quiet of three parts.
>
> The first was the ordinary quiet of a great house with too few people left in it: no boots in the
> upper hall, no fire in six of the seven grates, nobody calling down the stair for someone to bring
> a light. It is a quiet that takes centuries to make, and this one was very nearly finished.
>
> The second was smaller, and it was made on purpose. Two at the long table, and neither of them
> speaking of the road, or of the year, or of the seal on the box between them. One of the two had
> been not-speaking of it for a very long time.
>
> The third lay underneath the other two and was the reason for them, and it was the dry, patient,
> ledger-paper sound of a debt that has finished waiting.

> Between them on the table lay the record of the family, which had been begun in a good year by a
> man who could not have said why he was beginning it, and continued after him by four hundred
> people, of whom perhaps nine were honest.
>
> The last of the blood reached for the first page.
>
> The guardian said: *not that one. Start where it starts. Start with the night the hall was full.*

*[Cut to black. Hold four seconds — longer than is comfortable. Then 1042.]*

## II. THE PROLOGUE — year 1042

*Four minutes. Non-interactive but for the two choices in section III.*

### i. What there was, before any of it

> Before any of it there was a hall on a rise above a bend in the river Wend, and eleven farms that
> paid it, and a mill, and a village of a hundred and twenty souls who owed the house four days'
> work a year and gave them grudgingly and on the wrong days. There was a gatehouse, and the
> gatehouse had a gate, and the gate had been shut once, during a trouble that nobody now remembers,
> and had stood open in all weathers ever since.
>
> That is the whole of what the family had, and it is worth setting down plainly, because every
> account written afterwards begins later.

### ii. The man

> He is not named here. He has a name — it is in the parish roll at Wick in a hand that slopes, and
> it will be scraped out of three later documents by people who believed they were being kind — but
> the chronicle does not use it, and neither will this.
>
> What can be said is that he was forty years old that autumn; that he had a wife out of a house
> that had sold him three generations of maternal records and known exactly what it was selling; and
> a son of eleven who was frightened of the mill and would not say so, and who was carried up to bed
> that night by a mother who knew he was too old to be carried and carried him anyway.

### iii. The hall, full

> It was the last night of the Ingathering, and the hall was full in the way a hall is full perhaps
> twice in a life, and for most of the people standing in it that night was the once.
>
> There was a fire in all seven grates. There had never been, and there would not be again for two
> hundred years, and the light of seven fires went up into rafters that had been smoke-dark since
> before the family had a name, and found the carved bosses there, and the small crowded faces cut
> into them by a man whose own name is gone, and gave them back for a few warm hours the expressions
> he had meant them to have.
>
> It smelled of applewood and of wet wool steaming, of new bread and old honey and tallow and the
> sweet cidery rot of the last windfalls carried in from the orchard. The trestles ran the length of
> the room end to end, dressed in the good linen, which was not good, and which everybody agreed was
> beautiful. The tenants ate at the low tables until the light went and then went on eating by the
> fires, which was better. Children slept underneath the benches with the dogs against their backs
> and were stepped over all evening and did not wake. The Wayfolk had come up from Bramme and stayed
> three days and were paid in bread and a floor, and gave back a great deal more than they were paid,
> as they always did and were never once thanked for.
>
> Somebody sang the long stave about the drowned steward, badly, and was made to sing it again; and
> the second time the hall came in on the turn of it, the tenants and the Wayfolk and the reeve who
> could not hold a note and knew it, so that for the length of one verse there was nobody in that
> room who was not singing.
>
> Nobody thought to remark on any of it, because a thing that is happening is never the thing anyone
> remarks on. Of all the people at the low tables that night, the youngest would live long enough to
> see the roof of the west wing begin to leak, and not one of them would ever again stand in a room
> with all seven fires lit.

*[This is the Age's beauty, and it is spent in the prologue on purpose. Every later mention of a
full hall is measured against this one.]*

### iv. What came

> The dogs went out into the yard. Nobody had opened the door for them, and nobody could afterwards
> agree on how they had got out, and they stood in the wet with their backs to the hall and would
> not come in for any call or any meat; and the oldest of them, who had slept under that table for
> nine years and had never in his life declined to look at a door, did not look at the house at all.
>
> The knife by his hand went to rust between one sentence and the next.
>
> There was a smell of wet ash in a room where nothing had been burned in three days.
>
> The singing did not stop. That is the part the family has never been able to account for and has
> therefore never written down — that the hall went on being warm and loud and full, and that
> whatever else was true of that room, it was true at one end of it only.
>
> He saw something at the end of the table, though *something* is a word that assumes a great deal.
> The candles behind it were not blocked. They seemed instead to have forgotten that they had ever
> been lit.

*[The counterparty appears on screen exactly twice in a thousand years. This is the first. It has
no dialogue, no stated intent, no described shape, and it will not be explained.]*

### v. Three given

> And three things were given, and the giving of them took no time at all, and he was able to repeat
> all three afterwards — exactly, in order, without effort — which he found more frightening than
> anything else about that night.
>
> **The first was the blood.** Something in him, and in the son asleep upstairs, and in every child
> of that son forever: which was in no other family in the world and would be in no other, and which
> he did not understand well enough to refuse.
>
> **The second was the years.** A thousand of them, counted from that day, and the day counted.
>
> **The third was the keeping.** That the house would stand, and the farms would pay, and the mill
> would turn, so long as the family held what it had been given that night, and held it in a hand
> that could be shown.

### vi. Three owed

> And three things were owed, ascending.
>
> **The first he wrote down that night**, in a steady hand, and it has survived every fire and every
> reordering of the archive since, and the family has never once been without it.
>
> **The second he wrote down eight days later**, from memory, and got wrong in a way that would not
> matter for four hundred years, and then would.
>
> **The third he did not write down at all.**

*[Render the third as the chronicle renders an omission: a dated blank line, greyed, in the
document's own margin style — before the player has been taught what a dated blank line means. This
is the first thing in the game they will screenshot, and the Record mechanic in section VI is
introduced immediately afterwards, so the connection lands within the hour.]*

## III. THE FIRST CHOICES

*Two. Both echo for a thousand years and neither is presented as a strategy.*

### The house takes a name

> A house is not its hall. A house is a word that other people use about you when you are not in the
> room, and until tonight nobody has had cause to use one.

`> ______` *(the player types it. This word is the last word of the game.)*

### What his hand was on

> Three things had lain on that table all evening and he had not thought about any of them once. When
> it was over he found that his hand was on one, and would not easily come off it, and the family has
> told the story that way ever since — which is not the same as saying it happened that way.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The seal** | Nine-fold, brass, older than the hall. His father had used it for leases. It will be required for things that are not leases |
| **The ring** | Too large for him and too large for his son. It has fitted exactly two people in a thousand years and both of them were trouble |
| **The rod** | A yard of black wood with no marks on it. Nobody in the family has ever established what it is for, and everybody has had a theory |

*[All three are Regalia and all three must be held at once for Demigod. The choice does not decide
which one you own — it decides which one the family's story is about, and therefore which loss the
chronicle will find hardest to write around.]*

### Who was not forgiven

> Grudges are cheaper than land and they last a great deal longer, and this one was bought that same
> autumn and kept, like everything else the family kept, past all reason and past all use.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Marrow** | Sent a man in the summer to buy something the family had not yet been offered, and was refused, and has never accepted that it was second |
| **Calder** | Had the levy that year. Took two men off the eleven farms and returned neither, and sent no letter, and it was the not sending that did it |
| **Ilm** | Sold him a wife with three generations of notarised maternal records that will not survive close reading, and knew, and smiled at the signing |

## V. AGE ONE BEGINS — 1042

*The Age is not named. It will not be named for thirty years. The player is given weather and prices
and no title.*

> The years after went easily, and went on going easily, which is a harder thing to write about than
> a disaster and a harder thing to live inside than anyone expects.
>
> There was no war in the Nethering worth the name. The Warden at Cawdry died and was replaced by a
> cousin of the same opinions and the same table manners. Bread stayed at two mites and did not move,
> and the older tenants found this suspicious on principle, as they found every kindness that lasted
> longer than a season. Twice the Church sent a man up from the chapter house at Bramme to ask about
> the chapel that the family did not use, and twice he was fed well and walked to the road and sent
> back down the valley with nothing.
>
> And the mill on the Wend ran through eleven winters without freezing once.

## VI. THE TALE PICKS UP — the first generation

*Register changes here, and the player should feel it before they read it: two typefaces, and this
one is the plain one.*

> The reeve had been reeve for thirty years and he came up to the hall in the third winter to say
> that the wheel was still turning, and he said it the way a man says a thing he wants argued with.
>
> Nobody argued with him. Eilwen gave him small beer and asked after his daughter. He drank it
> standing, which he never did, and went back down the hill in the dark.
>
> The boy Aldous was fourteen and had stopped being frightened of the mill. He had started going
> down to watch it instead, in the cold, for an hour at a time, and could not say what he was
> watching for.

**The chronicler asks what to write.**

| | What descendants inherit |
|---|---|
| **Record** | *The wheel did not freeze, and we did not ask why.* The knowledge survives. So does the admission |
| **Omit** | Nothing. Eleven winters and a blank line, and in 1400 somebody stands in the same cold wondering why the family has no account of its own mill |
| **Embellish** | *The wheel was blessed at the Ingathering by a priest out of Bramme.* Respect now. A priest who can be looked up, in a register that survives, by anyone who ever wants to |

*[The first Record choice in the game is deliberately small and has no correct answer. Full event
bodies for this Age live in `packages/content/events/` and are authored to rothfuss-prose.]*

## VII. THE CLAUSE — entered in the contract's own hand

*The one the family has always had. No chronicler edits it; no Record choice is offered on it. It is
set in the third typeface, which is used for nothing else in the game.*

> The term is one thousand years from the day of signing, and the day of signing is counted. No
> extension is possible. No party may be absent at collection.

> Of the nine, this is the clause the family has never been without, and has read for a thousand
> years as a promise of time — as the one generous thing in a document that is generous nowhere else,
> and has been quoted at christenings, and embroidered, and set above a door. It is worth noting,
> since nobody in the house ever did, that it is not phrased as a grant of anything. It is phrased
> the way a man writes down an appointment he intends to keep.

*[Stage: **Wonder**. The clause establishes that a contract exists, that it is legally exact, and
that it is — read the way the family reads it — generous. The second sentence of the gloss is the
only place in Age 1 where the narrator's unreliability is visible, and it should read as an aside.]*

## VIII. INTERLUDE I — year 2042

*Ninety seconds. The first interlude is the longest; every one after this is shorter. Act one: the
disguise holds, and the player's question is why.*

> The page turns. It is the only sound in the house for some while.
>
> The last of the blood has read this part before — everyone does, it is the beginning — and reads it
> again the way a person rereads a thing while waiting for the courage to reach the middle.
>
> *The hall was full that night*, the record says, *and there was a fire in all seven grates.* It is
> one sentence about the best night the family ever had, and it is the longest the chronicle will
> ever dwell on anything good.
>
> The guardian says that there were nine dogs, and that the record says nothing about the dogs.
>
> The last of the blood asks how he knows that, and does not get an answer, and does not ask twice.
> It has been a long night already and there are four hundred years still to read.
>
> One thing more, and it is small, and it will not seem worth mentioning until it is much too late:
> the gate of the gatehouse is shut tonight. It is the second time.

*[The guardian is the founder. Nothing in this interlude says so and nothing for the next six Ages
will. On a second run every line above is a different line, which is the whole return on the frame.]*

*[The worsening clock starts here. The gate is the counter — shut in the prologue, shut tonight, and
in every subsequent interlude one further ordinary thing about Ardwen has stopped being ordinary.
Never explained, never remarked on by either speaker after this.]*

## IX. THE RUMOUR — what the world made of it

*One per Age, and the player watches it mutate.*

> The Wayfolk had a new stave out of the Nethering by 1061, and it was known by its first line, the
> way they all are.
>
> *There's a mill on the Wend that will not freeze.*
>
> In its first form it is four verses and a joke about a reeve, and the family is named, and the
> family comes off well. By 1120 the reeve is gone and there are two verses. By 1180 the house is
> unnamed, because a house that is still standing is bad luck to name in a song, and the mill has
> moved to Bramme, where there is no river.
>
> By the time anyone at Ardwen hears it sung back to them across their own table, it is a song about
> a bargain, and it is not clear which side of the bargain the family is supposed to have been on,
> and the man singing it has never been within forty miles of the Wend and is quite certain of every
> word.

**Feed it** *(cheap · Respect up · the Discrepancy deepens)* — **Correct it** *(expensive · Respect
down now · the exposure closes)* — **Leave it** *(it goes where songs go)*

## X. THE AGE IS NAMED — thirty years in

> It is the way of these things that a man knows what he has lived through only once it has begun to
> end, and the chroniclers of that century — who were mostly women with time that nobody else in the
> house had, working in a cold room off the stair — settled on a name for it late and without much
> argument.
>
> They called it **the Long Peace**, and they were not being kind. Peace is what the record calls the
> years in which the family got fat and got nothing: married cheaply because there was no urgency,
> buried its old people at the proper ages and in the proper ground, and finished the century with
> four more mouths at the table and no better line on the Roll of Houses than it had started with.
>
> The roof of the west wing began to leak in about 1090. It will be mentioned in every argument about
> money for the next nine hundred years.

---

## XI. THE AGE CLOSES

> And that was the whole of it: a hundred years of nothing happening to a family that something had
> already happened to.
>
> The mill ran. The farms paid. The gate stayed open. Somewhere in the middle of it a child of the
> blood woke early in front of witnesses at Wick, and three houses that had never written to Ardwen
> wrote to Ardwen inside the year, and the family learned, without ever once putting it into words,
> that what it had was worth something to people who did not know what it was.
>
> Few now remember that the family spent its first century believing it had been given a gift.

## Two notes for the build

**One divergence from the concepts document, and it is an improvement.** `DesignConcepts/eldritch-dynasty-story-concepts.md`
recommended a synod of two priests as the frame. The shipped content already has something better
and stranger — the guardian in `characters/founding.yaml` is the founder, he does not die, and he
is the player. The frame above uses that instead. The Testament concept's *interpretive pressure*
survives; its two scholars do not. If the synod is wanted, it belongs in the nested-tale layer as a
document the family buys, not at the table.

**Where each piece goes.** §I and §VIII are frame events (`events/frame.yaml`, tier `frame`). §II–IV
are the prologue, which has no home in content yet. §VII is `clause_of_the_term`, already written.
§IX needs the Rumour system. §V, §X and §XI are Age blurbs long-form — longer than the one-line
`blurb` field in `ages/ages.yaml` currently allows.
